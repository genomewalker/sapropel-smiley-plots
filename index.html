Recurring microbial roles across ancient Mediterranean deoxygenation

Author list and article reference to be added on publication.

Summary

Anthropogenic pressure is depleting the world’s oceans of oxygen, with uncertain consequences for marine ecosystems. Similar oxygen loss occurred in the geological past, driven by natural climate variability, and is recorded in marine sediments. However, fossils preserve little of the microorganisms that drive marine biogeochemical cycles, so we do not know whether the same taxa and metabolic functions return with each oxygen-depletion event. Here, we show that similar metabolic potential recurred across four Mediterranean sapropels despite substantial changes in species composition. These organic-rich sediment layers were deposited during episodes of oxygen depletion in the otherwise well-oxygenated Mediterranean Sea and span ~164 thousand years. We reconstructed their prokaryotic and viral communities using environmental DNA coupled with lipid biomarker analyses, authenticating ancient DNA by its post-mortem damage patterns. Picocyanobacteria occurred in all four events, but the leading genus tracked climate: Prochlorococcus was relatively more abundant in the warmer events, and Synechococcus in the coldest event. The symbiotic cyanobacterial diazotroph UCYN-A was a potential source of fixed nitrogen. Bacterial repertoires were especially similar among events, and the last-interglacial sapropel differed only through archaea that probably colonised the sediment after deposition. Together, these records show that similar microbial metabolic potential recurred across episodes of oxygen depletion under contrasting climates, providing a long-term benchmark against which present-day ocean deoxygenation can be compared.
